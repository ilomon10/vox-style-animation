# Research: Why railroads gave America time zones

Example project of the framework. Every line of the narration rests on a fact below; nothing else is claimed.
Checked 2026-11-16 by web search; sources disagree on small details, which are noted.

| # | Fact used in the script | Source(s) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Before standard time, towns kept their own local time set by the sun; noon in New York was not noon in Philadelphia. | Library of Congress, "Today in History: November 18" (read through a mirror: https://corpora.tika.apache.org/base/docs/govdocs1/424/424174.html); Library of Congress law blog, "Whose Time Is It, Anyway? A Brief History of Standardized Time Zones in the United States" (Nov 2024), https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2024/11/whose-time-is-it-anyway-a-brief-history-of-standardized-time-zones-in-the-united-states/ | The script says "towns across America", not "every town". |
| 2 | By one count, the railroads were scheduling trains on 56 different time standards by 1883. | ABA Journal, "U.S. railroads enact standard time zones", https://abajournal.com/magazine/article/us-railroads-enact-standard-time-zones | A single count: the script says "by one count". |
| 3 | On 18 November 1883 the railroads put four standard time zones for the continental United States into use ("the day of two noons"). | Library of Congress law blog (above); History Matters, George Mason University, https://historymatters.gmu.edu/d/5748; Union Pacific Railroad Museum, https://www.uprrmuseum.org/uprrm/exhibits/curators-corner/standard-time/index.htm | Some sources count five zones when they include the Intercolonial zone for eastern Canada; the script counts the four that cover the United States. |
| 4 | In October 1884 the International Meridian Conference in Washington, D.C. voted (22 to 1, San Domingo against, France and Brazil abstaining) to fix the prime meridian, the zero of longitude, at Greenwich. | Wikipedia, "International Meridian Conference", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Meridian_Conference; History Channel UK, "How Greenwich became the world's zero point", https://www.history.co.uk/articles/how-greenwich-became-the-worlds-zero-point | The vote was on 13 October; the resolutions were adopted on 22 October. The conference itself did not create time zones. |
| 5 | Congress made standard time zones federal law only in 1918, with the Standard Time Act (40 Stat. 450). | Library of Congress law blog (above) | |

Unverified or dropped: the exact minutes clocks moved in each city, and the claim that 18 November 1883 was "the only day with two noons".
