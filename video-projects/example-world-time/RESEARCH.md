# Research: How the world agreed on time

Example project of the framework: the block gallery in `examples/` was rendered from it. Every line of the narration
rests on a fact below; nothing else is claimed. Checked 2026-11-17 by web search.

| # | Fact used in the script | Source(s) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Before standard time, towns kept their own local time, set by the sun. | Library of Congress law blog, "Whose Time Is It, Anyway? A Brief History of Standardized Time Zones in the United States" (Nov 2024), https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2024/11/whose-time-is-it-anyway-a-brief-history-of-standardized-time-zones-in-the-united-states/ | Same fact as `example-time-zones` (RESEARCH 1). |
| 2 | By the 1870s railroads and telegraph lines crossed the continent: the first transcontinental telegraph line was completed on 24 October 1861, the first transcontinental railroad on 10 May 1869. | Engineering and Technology History Wiki, "Transcontinental Telegraph Line (U.S.)", https://ethw.org/Transcontinental_Telegraph_Line_(U.S.); HISTORY.com, "Transcontinental Railroad", https://www.history.com/topics/inventions/transcontinental-railroad | The script says "by the eighteen seventies", after both dates. |
| 3 | Sandford Fleming (1827–1915) was a railway engineer (chief engineer of the Canadian Pacific Survey from 1871) who promoted worldwide standard time. | Wikipedia, "Sandford Fleming", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandford_Fleming; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Sir Sandford Fleming", https://www.britannica.com/print/article/209975 | |
| 4 | Fleming proposed 24 time zones, each one hour (15 degrees of longitude) wide. | Wikipedia, "Sandford Fleming" (citing Fleming, *Terrestrial Time*, 1876, https://archive.org/details/cihm_06112); Linda Hall Library, "Sandford Fleming", https://lindahall.org/sandford-fleming | First in his 1876 memoir, revised in his 1879 papers: the script gives no year. |
| 5 | On 18 November 1883 American railroads adopted standard railway time in four zones: Pacific, Mountain, Central and Eastern. | Library of Congress law blog (above); the map itself: "Outline map with dials showing Standard Railway Time compared with Greenwich Mean Noon. Adopted November 18, 1883" (American Bank Note Co.), https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:PSM_V26_D162_Standard_railway_time_vs_greenwich_mean_time.jpg | Some sources count a fifth zone (Intercolonial, eastern Canada); the map shows four dials. |
| 6 | In October 1884 the International Meridian Conference in Washington adopted, unanimously, Resolution I: "That it is the opinion of this Congress that it is desirable to adopt a single prime meridian for all nations, in place of the multiplicity of initial meridians which now exist." | Wikipedia, "International Meridian Conference" (transcribing the Act of the Conference), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Meridian_Conference; *Nature* 31, 82 (1884), https://www.nature.com/articles/031082b0.pdf | The quote card shows the words "…to adopt a single prime meridian for all nations…", word for word. |
| 7 | Resolution II, the Greenwich meridian, passed 22 to 1 (San Domingo against), with France and Brazil abstaining. | Wikipedia, "International Meridian Conference" (above); History Channel UK, "How Greenwich became the world's zero point", https://www.history.co.uk/articles/how-greenwich-became-the-worlds-zero-point | Same fact as `example-time-zones` (RESEARCH 4). |
| 8 | The conference did not adopt time zones: Fleming's proposal for standard time was not put to a vote. | Wikipedia, "International Meridian Conference" (above) | |
| 9 | Today the world's time zones are set as offsets from Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). | Wikipedia, "Sandford Fleming" ("In the present day, UTC offsets divide the world into zones", citing *Smithsonian Magazine*); Wikipedia, "UTC offset", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTC_offset | |

Not used: the number of nations and delegates at the 1884 conference (sources give 25 or 26 nations, 40 or 41 delegates).
